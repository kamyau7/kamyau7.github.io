Ma On Shan Hike: Route and Trail Guide

Ma On Shan — "Saddle Mountain" — is the most photogenic ridge in Hong Kong and one of the few places where you can walk a genuine knife-edge for half an hour without ropes. It is also steeper and looser underfoot than its 702-metre height suggests, and the descent is where most people get into trouble.

The route described here climbs from Tai Shui Hang on the MTR, follows the ridge north over the summit, crosses the Hunchbacks and drops to Sai Kung town. That direction means the sharpest climbing comes while your legs are fresh and the long descent finishes at a bus terminus.

  • Distance: 10 km one way
  • Ascent: roughly 650 m
  • Time: 5–6 hours
  • Grade: 4 / 5
  • Start: Tai Shui Hang MTR, then walk to Kam Ying Court
  • Finish: Sai Kung town

What makes this ridge different

Most Hong Kong ridges are broad enough to walk two abreast. Ma On Shan is not. For roughly a kilometre either side of the summit the path is a narrow crest of loose stone with a drop on both sides — not vertical, but steep enough that a slip would be serious. There is no railing, no chain, and no alternative path.

That is the attraction and the risk in the same feature. If you are comfortable on exposed ground and have grippy shoes, it is exhilarating. If you are not, there is no shame in turning around at the saddle — the view from there is already most of the reward.

Ma On Shan Hiking Route: Section-by-Section Guide

1. Tai Shui Hang to the treeline (60–75 minutes)

From the estate at Kam Ying Court, a concrete path climbs through secondary forest. It is dull, shaded and steep, and it sets the tone: this route gains height fast. Ignore the temptation to rush — you will need the legs later.

2. The west ridge to the summit (75–90 minutes)

Above the trees the path becomes loose rock and short scrambles, with the summit pyramid ahead and Tolo Harbour behind you. This is the best photography on the route: the ridge line silhouetted against the water, with the New Territories towns spread out to the north.

3. The knife-edge (40–50 minutes)

Past the summit the path narrows to a crest. Walk it one at a time if your group is large, keep your weight over your feet, and do not stop in the middle of a loose section to take a photo. In wind above about force five this stretch stops being fun — turn back.

4. The Hunchbacks and descent to Sai Kung (120–150 minutes)

A series of four rounded humps follows, each a short climb and descent, before the trail drops steeply through grass and scrub towards Tai Shui Tseng and the edge of Sai Kung. The descent is long, hot and unshaded for the first hour. Refill at the village shops near the bottom.

Essential Gear for the Ma On Shan Hike

  • Shoes with deep tread. The ridge is loose stone on hard pan. Flat trainers slide on the descents.
  • Two litres of water minimum. There is nothing on the ridge, and the Sai Kung side is hot from noon.
  • A cap and sunglasses. Almost the whole upper route is exposed.
  • Gloves if you intend to use your hands on the scrambles — the rock is abrasive.
  • A phone with an offline map. The path is not always obvious where it crosses bare rock.

Ma On Shan Hike: Transport and Timing

Starting at Tai Shui Hang means the route begins at an MTR exit, which removes all transport uncertainty at the front end. At the finish, buses and minibuses from Sai Kung town run frequently to Diamond Hill and Choi Hung, well into the evening.

Start by 8am. A 10am start in November means finishing the descent in fading light, and the last hour of this route is not somewhere you want to be route-finding in the dark.

Best Time to Hike Ma On Shan

November to February is the season. December gives the clearest air and the coolest scrambling conditions. March and April are acceptable but often misty, which matters here because the route depends on seeing where the crest goes.

Do not attempt this in rain. Wet loose rock on a narrow crest is the exact combination that produces serious falls, and there is no easy exit from the middle section.

Frequently asked questions

Is Ma On Shan a scramble or a hike?

Both. About 80 per cent is steep walking; the ridge crest and two short sections near the summit require hands on rock. No ropes are needed in dry weather.

How does it compare with Sharp Peak?

Similar difficulty, different character. Sharp Peak has deeper sand and more sustained scrambling; Ma On Shan has a longer and more exposed crest. Both are grade 5 in the wrong conditions.

Can I shortcut back to the MTR?

Yes — from the saddle below the summit a side path drops back towards Kam Ying Court. It saves the ridge but not much time, and it is steep and loose.
