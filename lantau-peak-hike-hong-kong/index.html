Lantau Peak Hike: Route and Trail Guide

Lantau Peak is the second-highest mountain in Hong Kong at 934 metres, and the climb from Pak Kung Au is the steepest sustained ascent on any well-known trail in the territory. It is also the best place in the city to watch the sun come up — provided you start walking at four in the morning.

This page covers the classic one-way route: up from Pak Kung Au, across the summit, and down the stone staircase to the Big Buddha at Ngong Ping. It is the version worth doing, because it puts the hardest climbing first and ends somewhere with a bus, a café and a toilet.

  • Distance: 5.5 km one way
  • Ascent: roughly 600 m
  • Time: 3–3.5 hours
  • Grade: 4 / 5
  • Start: Pak Kung Au, on the Tung Chung to Mui Wo road
  • Finish: Ngong Ping, near the Tian Tan Buddha

Why the grade is 4 and not 3

Six hundred metres of climbing in under three kilometres is steep by any standard. What pushes Lantau Peak higher than its statistics suggest is the surface: the second half is unrelenting stone steps of uneven height, cut into a slope with no switchbacks to relieve the gradient. There is nowhere flat to recover, and nowhere to sit that is not a step.

Add exposure. Above about 700 metres the path leaves the scrub behind and runs along open rock, and the wind on the final ridge can be strong enough to make you lean. In summer this section is fully sunlit from mid-morning, which is precisely why the route is normally walked before dawn.

Lantau Peak Hiking Route: Section-by-Section Guide

1. Pak Kung Au to the ridge line (45–60 minutes)

The trailhead is a gap in the road between Tung Chung and Mui Wo, marked by a signboard and a small shelter. Steps begin immediately and do not stop. This first section is shaded by tall grass and low trees, which keeps it cooler but hides the view — do not judge the climb by it, and do not stop for long, because the mosquitoes here are persistent.

2. The open ridge (40–50 minutes)

Around 500 metres the vegetation thins out and the path bends onto the ridge itself. This is the section people remember: the ground falls away on both sides, the airport and the coast appear to the north, and on a clear winter morning the summit sits ahead of you as a dark triangle against the sky. Photograph here rather than at the top — the summit is often in cloud when the ridge below is clear.

3. The summit block (30–40 minutes)

The final 200 metres involves hands-on-rock in two short places and a stretch where the steps are cut directly into a steep slab. It is not technical, but it is not a stroll either. In wet weather this is where people turn around, and that is the correct decision.

4. The descent to Ngong Ping (60–75 minutes)

Descending towards the Big Buddha is a long staircase through bamboo and pine, and it is harder on the knees than the climb was on the lungs. Trekking poles earn their place here. Near the bottom the path opens onto the Po Lin Monastery grounds, where there are toilets, a vegetarian restaurant and buses to Tung Chung and Mui Wo.

The sunrise question

Lantau Peak is the most popular sunrise hike in Hong Kong, and the appeal is genuine: you climb in the dark, arrive as the sky lightens, and watch the sun come up over the South China Sea with the airport runway lit below you. But four things need to be true before you commit.

  • You start by 4am. Allow three hours in the dark, more if your group is slow. Missing the sunrise because you left at five is the commonest disappointment on this trail.
  • You have a proper head torch — not a phone. The steps are irregular and one hand is often needed for balance.
  • The forecast is clear. Cloud sits on this summit far more often than not. Check the Observatory's visibility reading before you set an alarm.
  • You have warm layers. The summit at 5am in January is close to freezing with wind chill, even though Tung Chung below will be fifteen degrees.

Lantau Peak Hike: Water, Facilities and Transport

There is no water and no toilet anywhere on the mountain. Both exist at Ngong Ping at the end, and neither exists at Pak Kung Au at the start. Carry two litres per person minimum, and finish your water on the way up rather than rationing it — dehydration on the descent is what makes people dizzy on the stairs.

Getting to Pak Kung Au: buses 3M, 11 and 23 run along the Tung Chung to Mui Wo road, and the stop is signposted. Getting away from Ngong Ping: bus 23 to Tung Chung, or the cable car if it is running and you are willing to queue.

Best Time to Hike Lantau Peak

November to February, without question. Those months bring dry air, temperatures in the low teens at the trailhead, and the clear visibility that makes the climb worthwhile. Avoid July and August entirely: the combination of full sun, high humidity and 600 metres of stairs is genuinely dangerous, and rescue call-outs on this route spike in the hot months.

Spring mornings often start clear and turn misty by nine. Autumn afternoons can be hazy. If you only have one day, pick a weekday in December.

Frequently asked questions

Is Lantau Peak harder than Sunset Peak?

Yes, noticeably. Sunset Peak is longer but gentler, with a broader summit plateau and more forgiving steps. Lantau Peak is shorter and steeper, with bare rock near the top.

Can a beginner do it?

Not as a first hike. Do Dragon's Back or the Peak loop first to learn how the heat and the steps affect you. As a third or fourth hike, in winter, with a steady pace, it is achievable.

Can I do it as an out-and-back?

You can, and many people do, but descending the same stone steps you just climbed is punishing. The one-way route to Ngong Ping is better in every way.
